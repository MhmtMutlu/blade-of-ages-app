Heroes

The 21 heroes of Blade of Ages: Çağların Kılıcı, each from a different myth and land. Every hero has their own weapon, play style and power.

  • Kaya "The Bull"

    Hunter-chief of Göbekli Tepe · brawny warrior

    Homeland
    Hill of Standing Stones
    Play style
    Close-range tank · shock wave · high health
    “Stone never forgets. Neither do I.”

    Kaya is a fictional hunter, inspired by the T-shaped pillars of Göbekli Tepe and the hunter-gatherer communities who raised them; the animals carved on those stones are his neighbours. His blade carries the memory of the hands that carved these stones. When the Tree calls, he hears it first: its lowest branch is his hill, and if the stones fade, everything above them shakes.

    In myth and history: About 11,000 years ago, hunter-gatherers raised T-shaped stones at Göbekli Tepe; Pillar 2 is carved with a bull, a fox and a crane. Göbekli Tepe

  • Nefertari

    Great Royal Wife · strategist

    Homeland
    Hourglass Desert
    Play style
    Wide orbit · Sun Shield · loot bonus
    “A letter travels farther than a thousand swords.”

    Nefertari is inspired by Nefertari, Great Royal Wife of Egypt. A letter sent in her name to the Hittite queen Puduhepa can still be read among the clay tablets of Hattusa. Her blade carries the memory of the messenger's road: a road that carried words, not swords. She answers the Tree because she knows that a peace made with words also fades when no one tells of it.

    In myth and history: Nefertari, Great Royal Wife of Ramesses II ("Naptera" in Hittite tablets), wrote letters to Hattušili III and Puduhepa; she sent Puduhepa a gold necklace of twelve strands and fine clothes. Nefertari

  • Muwatalli

    King of Kadesh · Great King of Hatti

    Homeland
    Gate of the Storm God
    Play style
    Chariot charge · lightning strike · mid range
    “The gods bear witness; I keep the count.”

    Muwatalli is inspired by Muwatalli II, the Hittite king who faced the Egyptian army at Kadesh with his chariots. In the game he is a king and a keeper of accounts: like the Hittite treaties that named the gods as witnesses, he records everything. His blade carries the memory of his chariot horses. He answers the Tree because if the mountains' tales fade, no one will know where lightning falls.

    In myth and history: In the 13th century BC, Muwatalli II met the army of Ramesses II at Kadesh; the battle had no clear winner, and the Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty was signed later, under Hattušili III. Battle of Kadesh Muwatalli II

  • Antiope

    Amazon of Themiscyra · athletic, defiant

    Homeland
    Forests of Themiscyra
    Play style
    Speed and evasion · twin short swords · crits
    “If you can't keep up, remember my trail.”

    Antiope is inspired by the Amazon Antiope of Greek myth. The myths place the Amazons' city, Themiscyra, on the Black Sea coast beside the Thermodon river. In the old tales the Amazons are often an obstacle on another hero's path; this Antiope runs her own. Her blade carries the memory of her sisters. She answers the Tree so that no one else tells her story for her.

    In myth and history: Antiope is named as a sister of the Amazon queens Hippolyte and Melanippe; Themiscyra is placed on the banks of the Thermodon, today the Terme river. Antiope Themiscyra

  • Aristos

    Hoplite of Olympus's Threshold · honourable, stubborn

    Homeland
    Threshold of Olympus
    Play style
    Phalanx shield · knockback · defensive
    “With the shield, or on it.”

    Aristos is a fictional hoplite, inspired by the foot soldiers of the ancient Greek cities and their great round shields. In the phalanx every shield also covers part of the man beside it; that is Aristos's whole philosophy. His blade carries the memory of the comrade who stood next to him in the line. He answers the Tree because one empty place in the line leaves every shield exposed.

    In myth and history: Hoplites carried a large round shield, the aspis; Thucydides writes that in the phalanx each soldier sheltered behind the shield of the man to his right (Peloponnesian War 5.71). Hoplite

  • Adapa

    Sage of Eridu · who turned down forever

    Homeland
    Eridu Shore
    Play style
    Hearth-fire whip · life spark · heals on hit
    “I turned down forever; being told is enough.”

    Adapa is inspired by the Mesopotamian tale "Adapa and the South Wind". The wise mortal of Eridu breaks the wings of the wind that capsized his boat, is summoned to heaven and, following advice, unknowingly refuses the bread of life. His blade carries the memory of Eridu's hearth fires. He answers the Tree because one who will not live forever knows best what being told means.

    In myth and history: "Adapa and the South Wind" is known from tablets found at Amarna (14th century BC) and in the library of Ashurbanipal; Adapa is the wise servant of Ea's temple at Eridu, and he does not eat the food of life he is offered. Adapa

  • Sigrún

    Valkyrie of the Rime Fjord · solemn

    Homeland
    Rime Fjord
    Play style
    Dive · wing shield · soul gathering
    “Speak your name. I will carry it.”

    Sigrún is inspired by the valkyrie of the Helgi poems in the Poetic Edda; her name reads as "victory rune". In the poems she leads nine valkyries. The game's Sigrún carries no one from battle; she carries names, every name she is told. Her blade carries the memory of the ships she guarded in the storm. She answers the Tree because, in the north, names are fading.

    In myth and history: Sigrún appears in Helgakviða Hundingsbana I and II in the Poetic Edda; when she first meets Helgi, she leads nine Valkyries; the first poem tells how she guards ships in a storm from the sky (not verified: the stanza has not been read). Sigrún Helgakviða Hundingsbana I

  • Fedelm

    Seer of the Oak Grove · poet, sage

    Homeland
    Oak Grove
    Play style
    Healing field · green oath · slows the horde
    “Every leaf remembers a name.”

    Fedelm is inspired by the poet-prophetess of the Irish epic Táin Bó Cúailnge. At its start she stops an army with her chariot, a weaver's beam in hand, and tells the queen exactly what she sees, wanted or not. Her blade carries the memory of her years learning prophecy in Alba. She answers the Tree because when no one says what they see, tales fall silent.

    In myth and history: At the start of the Táin Bó Cúailnge, Fedelm, a poet-seer (banfhili) of Connacht, rides her chariot up to Queen Medb's army and foresees how the war will go. Fedelm Táin Bó Cúailnge

  • Master Jin

    Wandering Master of the Cloud Temple · calm

    Homeland
    Cloud Temple
    Play style
    Parry master · 3× parry damage
    “The sword does not turn; the world turns around it.”

    Master Jin is a fictional wandering master, inspired by the jian, China's straight double-edged sword, and by the wuxia tradition of wandering swordsmen trained in mountain temples. He knows how to meet an attack better than how to make one. His blade carries the memory of every student he met on the road. He answers the Tree because a wanderer notices fading roads before anyone else.

    In myth and history: The jian is a straight, double-edged sword known in China for more than two thousand years, and it is traditionally called "the gentleman of weapons". Jian

  • Tomoe

    Onna-musha of the Cherry Temple · graceful, sharp

    Homeland
    Cherry Temple
    Play style
    Wide naginata sweep · counter · long reach
    “The blossom falls; the blade remembers.”

    Tomoe is inspired by Tomoe Gozen, the woman warrior of The Tale of the Heike. The tale remembers her as a fine archer, a fearless rider and a warrior worth a thousand, though whether she truly lived is still debated. Her blade carries the memory of the horses she rode in the Kiso mountains. She answers the Tree because someone has to remember the falling blossom.

    In myth and history: No primary records of the Genpei War (1180–1185) mention Tomoe Gozen; the Heike Monogatari tells of her as an onna-musha who fought beside Minamoto no Yoshinaka. Tomoe Gozen Onna-musha

  • Itzel

    Hunter of the Ceiba Forest · silent, patient

    Homeland
    Ceiba Forest
    Play style
    Silent strike · night stealth · trail (damage over time)
    “The sun sets, and I rise.”

    Itzel is a fictional Maya hunter, inspired by the Twins of the Popol Vuh, who brought down Seven Macaw, the bird that called itself the sun, with a blowgun, and by the ceiba, the tree linking underworld, earth and sky in the Maya world. Her blade carries the memory of the Twins' patience. She answers the Tree because morning still has not come to her forest.

    In myth and history: In the Popol Vuh, Hunahpu strikes Vucub Caquix, who thought he was the sun, with a blowgun; in Maya tradition, the ceiba is the sacred tree that links the layers of the world. Maya Hero Twins Vucub Caquix Ceiba pentandra

  • Tomyris

    Queen of the Massagetae · beyond the steppe

    Homeland
    Steppe of the Massagetae
    Play style
    Mounted speed · wolf summoning · arrow rain
    “The sky sees, the steppe remembers.”

    Tomris is inspired by Tomyris, queen of the Massagetae in Herodotus's Histories. When King Cyrus of Persia wanted her land, Herodotus says, she told him to be content with his own kingdom and leave hers alone. The Massagetae rode the steppe beyond the river Herodotus calls the Araxes. Her blade carries the memory of the steppe's horses. She answers the Tree because the steppe writes its word on the wind.

    In myth and history: Everything we know of Tomyris comes from Herodotus (Histories I.205–214); he describes the Massagetae as a steppe people living to the east, beyond the Araxes. Which river Herodotus meant by the Araxes is still debated. Tomyris Massagetae

  • Rostam

    Champion of Zabulistan · under the Simurgh's wing

    Homeland
    Mountain of the Simurgh
    Play style
    Champion's mace · seven-trials combo · overwhelming power
    “I passed seven gates; you are the eighth.”

    Rostam is inspired by the great champion of Ferdowsi's Shahnameh. In the epic, Rostam and his horse Rakhsh pass through seven hard trials, the Haft Khan, and the giant bird Simurgh, who raised his father Zal in her nest, watches over the family. His blade carries the memory of Rakhsh, the horse that never left him alone on any road. He answers the Tree because this is the eighth trial.

    In myth and history: In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (around 1010), the seven trials Rostam faces with Rakhsh are known as the "Haft Khan"; the Simurgh raises Rostam's father, Zal, in her nest. Rostam Rakhsh

  • Arjuna

    Archer of Kurukshetra · wielder of Gandiva

    Homeland
    Plain of Kurukshetra
    Play style
    Long-range arrows · time-slowing aim · multiple targets
    “I don't see the target, only the bird's eye.”

    Arjuna is inspired by the great archer of the Indian epic, the Mahabharata. As the story goes, his teacher Drona shows his students a wooden bird on a branch and asks what they see; Arjuna answers that he sees only the bird's eye. His blade carries the memory of Drona's lessons. He answers the Tree because even focus scatters when no one tells of it.

    In myth and history: In the Mahabharata, Arjuna is the third of the Pandava brothers and the owner of the bow Gandiva; he is remembered as Drona's star pupil. The bird's-eye scene is common in popular retellings; its place in the critical edition has not been verified. Arjuna Gandiva

  • Vasilisa

    Lantern-Bearer of the Beech Forest · East Slavic tale

    Homeland
    Beech Forest
    Play style
    Fire lantern · guiding doll (auto-target) · burning
    “Darkness is for those without a lamp.”

    Vasilisa is inspired by "Vasilisa the Beautiful", an East Slavic tale collected by Alexander Afanasyev. Helped by a little doll her mother left her, Vasilisa goes deep into the forest to Baba Yaga's house, finishes every task and brings fire home. Her blade carries the memory of her mother's blessing on the doll. She answers the Tree because someone must always carry a lantern in the dark.

    In myth and history: "Vasilisa the Beautiful" appears in Afanasyev's collection Narodnye russkie skazki (1855–1863); white, red and black riders, standing for day, the sun and night, pass by Baba Yaga's house. Vasilisa the Beautiful

  • Adeola

    Wind Runner of the Niger Shore

    Homeland
    Niger Shore
    Play style
    Whirlwind orbit · flings enemies · speed boost
    “The wind doesn't ask; it passes.”

    Adeola is a fictional runner, inspired by the banks of the Niger, West Africa's great river, and by the stories of wind and water told there for generations; her name is Yoruba. Her twin blades carry the memory of the shore wind that taught her to run. She answers the Tree because a story the wind does not carry from shore to shore is stranded on the way.

    In myth and history: Adeola is not a figure from myth. The Niger runs about 4,180 km across West Africa: it rises in Guinea, flows through Mali, Niger and Nigeria, and empties into the Gulf of Guinea. Niger River

  • Tama

    Forest Warrior of Aotearoa

    Homeland
    Isle of Greenstone
    Play style
    Ally power (area buff) · mere strike · defense
    “My ancestors are with me; who is with you?”

    Tama is a fictional warrior, inspired by the mere, the short, flat club of the Māori tradition of Aotearoa (New Zealand). His name means "son" or "young man" in te reo Māori. His mere carries the memory of his ancestors, whose names he can recite one by one. He answers the Tree because when ancestors' names go untold, the road is forgotten.

    In myth and history: The mere is a short, flat club that Māori warriors used in close combat; the most prized were made of greenstone (pounamu), were often named, and were passed down as family heirlooms and signs of chiefly authority. Mere (weapon)

  • Qori

    Khipu Reader of Qorikancha

    Homeland
    Qorikancha
    Play style
    Sun mark (damage boost) · khipu chain · binding
    “Every knot is a name.”

    Qori is a fictional khipu reader, inspired by the khipu, the Andean system of keeping records with knotted coloured cords, and by Qorikancha, the gold-covered temple of Cusco; her name means "gold" in Quechua. Her blade carries the memory of the first cord she learned to read. She answers the Tree because every knot is a name, and a knot untied is a name lost.

    In myth and history: The khipu was an Inca record-keeping system that stored numbers and information as knots tied in coloured cords; Qorikancha (Coricancha) is the Inca temple in Cusco. Quipu Coricancha

  • Lucius

    Centurion of the Legion · Rome's iron discipline

    Homeland
    Legion Camp
    Play style
    Testudo shield · pilum throw · battle line
    “The line does not break; we do.”

    Lucius is a fictional centurion, inspired by the centurions of the Roman legions and by the testudo, the formation in which soldiers locked their shields together like a tortoise's shell. His blade carries the memory of the eight comrades who shared his tent. He answers the Tree because when the line is not held the road is lost, and when the road is lost no one gets home.

    In myth and history: A centurion was an officer in the Roman army who led a unit of about eighty men; in the testudo, shields were joined at the front and overhead to form a shell. Centurion Testudo formation

  • Yushin

    Young Master of the Hwarang · flower knight of Silla

    Homeland
    Hwarang Mountain
    Play style
    Flower-sword combo · charged power strike · agility
    “A flower lasts a season; honour lasts forever.”

    Yushin is inspired by the Hwarang, the "flowering youth" of the Silla kingdom, and by General Kim Yu-sin, who became a hwarang at fifteen; but he is not Kim Yu-sin himself. The Hwarang travelled the mountains and learned poetry, music and the sword. His blade carries the memory of the flowering youths he walked with. He answers the Tree because only being told makes a one-season blossom last forever.

    In myth and history: Kim Yu-sin (595–673) became a hwarang at fifteen and was the general who led Silla's unification of the Korean peninsula; his life is told in the Samguk Sagi. Kim Yu-sin Hwarang

  • Unutuş

    Traveller of the Broken Clock

    Homeland
    The Broken Clock
    “My clock is broken. I only tell stories.”

    Unutuş is a traveller from the Broken Clock. Unutuş does not remember a name; with a broken clock, there is no growing older and no growing up. The hourglass crown and staff share the colour of the violet light deep in the Tree's roots. In the game Unutuş is unlocked through the story, and the biography fills in realm by realm. Who is Unutuş? That story is still being written.

    In myth and history: Unutuş is taken from no myth: Unutuş belongs to the Tree of Ages, this game's own tale.
